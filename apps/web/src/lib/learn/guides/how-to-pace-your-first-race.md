## The mistake everyone makes once

In nearly every first race, the same thing happens: the gun goes, the adrenaline surges, the crowd pulls you along, and you run the first kilometre far faster than you have ever trained. It feels effortless — for about ten minutes. Then the bill comes due, and the back half of the race becomes a grim survival shuffle. Almost every blown-up first race traces back to going out too fast.

The fix is simple to say and hard to do: **start slower than feels right.**

## Even splits win

The most efficient way to race a distance is to run it at a roughly even pace from start to finish — even splits. A small negative split (the second half slightly faster than the first) is the gold standard, and it is only possible if you hold back early. Banking time at the start by sprinting never works; the energy you spend going out too fast costs you far more later than it ever saves.

Know, before the start, the pace you can realistically sustain for the whole distance — not your best-ever single kilometre, but a pace you could hold for the full race. Then run the first few kilometres a touch *under* that, and let the effort build.

## Course recon

A little homework pays off:

- **Know the elevation.** A hill at kilometre eight changes how you should run kilometre one. Run the climbs by effort, not pace — ease back going up and let it come back on the way down.
- **Know where the turns and surfaces change** so nothing surprises you.
- **Run part of the course beforehand** if you can, or at least study the route. Familiarity removes a layer of race-day stress.

## Run by effort, not just the watch

The watch is a guide, not a dictator. On a hot day, into a headwind, or up a climb, the same pace costs more effort — so let pace drift and hold the *effort* steady. Learning to read your own effort is the skill that separates a controlled race from a blow-up.
