## Consistency is the whole game

The runner who improves is not the one who has occasional heroic weeks — it is the one who runs a sensible amount, week after week, for months. A repeatable weekly routine you can actually sustain beats any amount of all-or-nothing enthusiasm. The goal of this guide is to help you build a week you can keep.

## How often to run

For a new runner, **three runs a week** is the sweet spot: enough to build fitness and form a habit, with rest days between to absorb the work. As you adapt, four or five becomes manageable. More is not automatically better — adding runs faster than your body can adapt is the classic route to injury. Add one run a week at most when you step up, and only once the current load feels easy.

## Balance easy and hard

The biggest mistake recreational runners make is running every session at the same moderate-hard effort — too hard to recover from, too easy to drive real adaptation. The fix is the principle every good plan is built on: **most of your running should be easy.**

A sensible week mixes:

- **Easy runs** — conversational pace, the bulk of your week. These build your aerobic base, the foundation everything else sits on.
- **One harder session** — once you have a base, a single faster or longer effort each week (intervals, a tempo run, or a longer easy run) provides the stimulus to improve.
- **The long run** — gradually building the duration of one run a week is what unlocks distance.

If you only remember one thing: slow your easy runs down so you can push your one hard session properly.

## Recovery is training

Rest days are not lost days — they are when the body actually rebuilds stronger than before. Skipping them does not make you fitter; it makes you tired and injured. Take at least one or two full rest days a week as a beginner, and treat sleep and easy nutrition as part of the plan, not extras.

## Let a plan carry the structure

Holding all of this in your head — how far, how easy, when to push, when to rest, how to ramp safely — is exactly the kind of structure a training plan provides. A plan lays out each week so every session has a purpose and the volume builds at a safe rate, which means you can stop second-guessing and just run the day in front of you.
